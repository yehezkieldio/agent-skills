# Anti-formulaic writing screen

Run every draft against this list before presenting it, regardless of composition type — a technical article, a conceptual essay, or an academic writeup all fail in the same handful of ways once a piece gets long enough that a repeatable rhetorical template is easier to reach for than a fresh sentence. Most of these patterns aren't unique to AI-generated text; they're what any writer's prose degrades toward under time pressure or over a long draft. They just show up with unusual reliability in model output, which is why this list exists. This is a screening checklist, not a style guide to follow positively — it names patterns to remove, not patterns to add.

Prefer direct, non-formulaic prose. Screen out:

- **Em dashes** used as a substitute for a real sentence break.
- **Fragment-triplet negation** — "Not a X. Not a Y. A Z." (or similarly clipped runs of two or three negated sentence fragments landing on one affirmative). Reads as a rhetorical flourish rather than an argument; state the affirmative claim directly instead.
- **Negation pivots** — "it's not X, it's Y" as a reflexive sentence shape used repeatedly. This is the single most common tell across a full draft: it shows up as "That's not X. It's Y," "None of that is X. It's Y," "isn't X, it's Y," and "isn't X. It's Y" scattered across nearly every section. One or two per piece is fine when they land the actual thesis; more than that reads as a rhetorical tic used to manufacture "nuance" rather than a genuine correction of a real misconception. Default to a plain declarative sentence instead of clearing away a misreading first — trust the reader. This pattern is stubborn: a pass that fixes the most obvious instances (a negated section title, a "that's not X, it's Y" clause) leaves the underlying reflex intact in subtler forms — "is not, by itself, a meaningful X," "not the same kind of thing as," "do not manufacture Y." After any negation-pivot cleanup pass, re-grep specifically for "not," "isn't," "doesn't," and "don't" near the start of a sentence and check each one for whether the point could be stated as a direct positive claim instead.
- **Section titles phrased as a negation** — "Not Complexity for Its Own Sake," "Not Just Noise." A title built on rejecting a strawman before naming the real subject is a negation pivot at the structural level, not just the sentence level. Name the section for what it actually claims.
- **Comparative negation as a definitional device** — "less a technology than a discipline," "less a data structure than a record," "present from the beginning, rather than restrictions bolted on later." Defining a thing by subtracting it from a nearby alternative, instead of just naming what it is, is the negation pivot's quieter cousin — it survives negation-pivot cleanup passes because it doesn't use "not" or "isn't." State the target noun or property directly ("the state machine amounts to operational discipline," "a state machine acts as a historical record") and cut the comparative scaffolding around it. This one is persistent across revision rounds: after fixing the obvious "X isn't Y, it's Z" cases, a piece tends to keep explaining *mechanisms* by contrasting them against a rejected alternative — "what makes this genuine, rather than a shortcut, is...", "reducing consequences, rather than making each one faster", "mattered more than making it faster". Grep for "rather than" and "instead of" specifically at the point a mechanism is being defined (not just anywhere in a sentence) and check whether the definition survives with the contrast clause deleted; it almost always does, and reads more confident without it.
- **"It's worth [verb]ing" crutch** — "it's worth admitting," "it's worth pausing on," "it's worth being honest about," "it's worth stating plainly." A tell for transitioning into a "thoughtful" register instead of just making the point. Cut the phrase and state the point directly.
- **Negative-absolute boundary setters** — "None of that is X," "none of this needs Y," used reflexively to pre-empt a misunderstanding before making the real claim. Vary with "Admittedly," "Granted," a semicolon fold, or just state the affirmative claim and let it stand.
- **Wall-to-wall aphoristic paragraph endings** — every paragraph closing on a perfectly balanced, quotable one-liner creates fatigue over a full-length piece. Real writing has more mundane, practical, or transitional paragraph endings; save the aphorism for section-ending or piece-ending moments, not every 100–150 words. This is one of the hardest patterns to fully clear: even after several rounds of cutting the most obvious slogans, a closing sentence like "Partial truth, honestly scoped, is a better property for a system to have than fake completeness that only reveals its limits when someone finally notices" can survive because it's phrased as an analytical comparison rather than an obvious one-liner. If a section- or paragraph-final sentence resolves into a tidy "X, [qualifier], is better/worse than Y" or similar comparative shape, that's the mic-drop pattern wearing analytical clothing — end on the plain technical fact instead ("partial truth, honestly scoped, tells a caller exactly where a representation's authority ends").
- **Standalone aphorism openers, not just closers** — a section or paragraph leading with a single flat maxim before any supporting material ("Many things may depend on truth, and very few things should be permitted to define it," followed only then by the actual argument) bookends the same way a mic-drop closer does, just at the other end. Fold the insight into the body where it's earned by the surrounding sentences instead of announcing it up front as a standalone declaration — a strong point argued well doesn't need a maxim on either side of it to land. Note: if a maxim like this was specifically requested as one of the piece's core stated opinions, don't cut the idea — restyle its placement (mid-paragraph, earned by argument around it) rather than deleting the substance, and flag the change back to whoever requested it, since a later style pass and an earlier content requirement can genuinely conflict.
- **Sermonizing or directive imperatives aimed at the reader** — "Do not merely make redundant work cheaper. Question why the work exists," "Trust the reader," "Ask yourself whether..." Switching from describing the system's own design choices to issuing the reader a command breaks the essay's observational register and reads as a lecture. Keep the imperative's content but restate it as a description of what the system (or the design choice) does or eliminates, not an instruction to the person reading ("collapsing five mutations into one recomputation eliminates the redundant work entirely, rather than just making each occurrence of it cheaper").
- **Disarming meta-commentary that precedes a claim** — a preamble that frames the sentence about to be made as uncomfortable, surprising, or easy to misjudge before making it ("There's an uncomfortable consequence of this preference:", "That asymmetry is the entire point, though it's easy to overstate into something broader than it is"). This is a softer version of essayistic meta-commentary (see above) aimed specifically at pre-disclaiming a claim's force rather than commenting on the writing itself. Cut the preamble and state the claim; the claim's own content should carry whatever weight or nuance the preamble was trying to signal in advance. Watch for this same move surviving a rewrite in a subordinate clause rather than a full sentence — restructuring "That asymmetry is the entire point, though it's easy to overstate" into "..., which is the part easiest to overstate into something broader than it is" removes the standalone sentence but keeps the exact same hedge; the fix is deleting the hedge's content, not just its sentence boundary.
- **Signpost blockquotes carrying no new information** — a breather interlude (see SKILL.md's long-form pacing guidance) earns its place only when it adds a concrete detail or a genuinely fresh angle. A blockquote that just restates the preceding paragraph's point in punchier language ("Pause here for a second: most of what follows rests on this trade," "A system willing to admit where it actually runs tends to be more honest about everything else in it, too") is a signpost wearing a breather's clothes — cut it rather than keep it for pacing's sake. Keep an interlude only if removing it would delete real content, not just tone.
- **Blockquotes as a device at all, once the piece has drifted toward using them as slogan-boxes** — even a single surviving blockquote reads as visually inconsistent once every other one has been cut for being a signpost, and it invites the same "pull-quote" instinct to creep back in on the next revision. If a draft's blockquote habit turns out to be mostly signposting rather than genuine breathing room (check by asking whether the sentence loses anything by being folded back into its paragraph as plain text), the simpler fix is dropping blockquotes as a device for that piece entirely rather than curating down to "the one good one."
- **Essayistic meta-commentary about the section or the essay itself** — a sentence whose subject is the *writing*, not the *engineering*: "Hanging an entire section on X sounds like a stretch," "Not every escape hatch here is drawn this cleanly" as a lead-in that frames what follows as a confession, "this section's own title working backwards." These stage a moment of authorial self-awareness that reads as manufactured humility rather than genuine argument. State the technical fact directly (the flag ships permissive by default) instead of first commenting on how well or poorly it fits the argument being made around it.
- **"A [noun] is a [noun]" / definitional sentence openers** — starting paragraphs with a flat definitional claim ("A status column is a small thing...", "Forward construction is the ordinary case...") is a common default that gets monotonous in bulk. Vary openers: lead with a verb, a gerund, an imperative, or a conjunction instead of restating the subject as a definition.
- **Colon elaboration** — "the result was clear: everything broke" as a tic, overused as a paragraph-ending move.
- **Setup-and-delivery colon punctuation** — splitting a sentence into a broad claim followed by an emphatic reveal via colon as a reflexive habit ("...a single claim before going any further: small infrastructure does not produce small software," "it also buys something concrete: the job can be enqueued..."). One or two per piece read as a deliberate cadence choice; a colon doing this same "wind-up, then land it" job five or six times across a draft reads as a formula. Vary with a period break, or an explicit conjunction (*because*, *by*, *that*) instead.
- **Rhetorical Q&A** — "So what does this mean? It means..."
- **Rhetorical-question section headers or openers** — a heading phrased as a question ("Where Does Complexity Live?", "What Doesn't It Know?"), or a section opening with "The obvious next question is..." Reads as a lecture-deck transition device rather than an argument continuing under its own momentum. Use a declarative title that states the section's actual claim or subject, and open by continuing the argument directly rather than staging a question-and-answer beat.
- **Staccato cadence** — long runs of short sentences with no variation, mistaken for punchiness.
- **Hedge stacking** — "might potentially somewhat suggest."
- **Anaphora** — repeating the same sentence opener across a paragraph for rhythm rather than argument.
- **"The X is still Y. The A is still B, still C" parallel-decay pattern** — a severe, specific case of anaphora that shows up especially in closings: a short run of near-identical "[noun] is still [state]" clauses about closely related nouns, each one adding less real content than the last ("The machine is still low-end. The server is still small, still complaining about the same thing it always complained about."). It reads as manufactured wistfulness rather than an actual observation. If a closing wants to return to an earlier image, do it in one full sentence that says something new, not a string of "still" clauses padding out the same idea.
- **Fragment-pair "quotable" endings forced for their own sake** — closing on two short parallel fragments because it sounds like an ending ("The machine got faster over the years. The instinct never did.") rather than because the piece's actual final thought is genuinely that compressed. A forced version of this reads as reaching for a mic-drop rather than earning one. Prefer a real, complete closing paragraph — see the closing conventions in `type-index.md` and the chosen type's own file — over manufacturing a punchy fragment pair.
- **Exact triples** — reflexively grouping things in threes ("no X, no Y, no Z") as a default list shape rather than because three genuinely is the count.
- **Cliché intensifiers** — "incredibly," "truly," "fundamentally" (unless load-bearing), "absolutely."
- **Elevated register** — reaching for a more formal word than the sentence needs.
- **Filler adverbs** — "essentially," "basically," "simply," used as padding.
- **Throat-clearing openers** — "In this article, we will..." / "Let's dive into..." / "What follows is..." / "This is an examination of..." / "This is a story about...". Jump directly into the subject instead of labeling the piece before it starts.
- **False conclusion signposts** — "In conclusion," "Ultimately," "At the end of the day," when nothing is actually being concluded.
- **Connector-heavy paragraphs** — "Moreover," "Furthermore," "Additionally," chaining sentences that don't need chaining.
- **Forced balanced takes** — "on one hand... on the other hand" when the piece actually has a clear position.
- **Staged vulnerability** — performative admissions of struggle inserted for effect rather than because they're true and relevant ("I'll be honest, this was hard for me").
- **Predictable metaphors** — reaching for the first available comparison (journeys, puzzles, building blocks) instead of a precise technical description. Also watch overall metaphor density across a long piece — even fresh metaphors ("a snapshot wearing a read model's clothes," "curdles into negligence," "a slow-motion data-loss bug") stack up and read as over-written if every paragraph has one. Let some paragraphs stay in plain, literal technical language.
- **AI-favored vocabulary** — specific words that models reach for far more than the average technical writer does: "substrate" (say "database," "system," or "foundation" instead unless the abstraction genuinely needs that word), "epistemic," "liability," "axis," "load-bearing," "mechanical sympathy" as filler rather than a deliberate named citation. One deliberate use of a term like this in a long piece is a citation; three or four is a tell.
- **"Claim" as connective filler** — once a piece has removed a hedge word like "narrower" per the item above, "claim" itself becomes the next word to lean on as its replacement ("the actual claim is...", "the claim here is...", "a narrower and more defensible claim"). Watch for it specifically after doing a hedge-word cleanup pass — the underlying reflex (narrow the assertion before making it) survives even when the specific word that carried it gets fixed, and just finds a new word to live in. State the claim; don't narrate that you're making one.
- **"Actually" and "real/really" as reflexive intensifiers** — cheap emphasis added out of habit rather than because the sentence needs it ("what actually differs," "actually gets checked," "actually running inside"). A long piece can accumulate twenty or more of these without anyone noticing while writing straight through. After a full draft, grep for both and cut every instance that doesn't change the sentence's meaning when removed — most won't.
- **The "most systems/most people" section opener** — starting a section by describing how the industry or most engineers handle a problem before revealing how this system handles it ("Most systems treat observability as an emission problem...", "There's a common shape to how systems get designed...", "There's a version of performance work that makes every operation cheaper..."). One use is a legitimate contrast; nearly every section doing it turns a real argument into a repeatable template where the payoff is always "and this is different." Open with the concrete mechanism directly and let the implicit contrast with common practice stay unstated — the reader can infer what's unusual about an approach from the approach itself, without being told first what it isn't.
- **Symmetrical binary comparisons** — pairing two concepts into a strict two-clause parallel structure ("X tells you A. Y tells you B.", "Detection is cheap and mechanical: [test]. Diagnosis is harder and only sometimes mechanical: [test]."). Reads as a slide-deck comparison table converted to prose. When a section wants to distinguish two things, let the distinction emerge across a full sentence or two of continuous prose with actual subordination (because, since, while) instead of two matched declarative clauses back to back.
- **Any single hedge or qualifier word, overused as a tic** — this isn't limited to the specific words above. Any word that does the job of "let me narrow this claim before I state it" — "narrower," "specifically," "precisely," "strictly" — will get reached for reflexively once a piece leans on the move of qualifying claims before stating them, and a long draft can rack up five or six uses without anyone noticing while writing straight through. After a full draft, grep the piece for its own suspiciously frequent words (not just the ones on this list) and cut any single word appearing more than two or three times doing the same rhetorical job — vary the phrasing each time instead of reusing the same qualifier.
- **The same illustrating example reused across many sections without adding anything new** — a real mechanism from the codebase, once found, is easy to reach for again in every later section it's tangentially relevant to. If section four introduces a mechanism in full and sections seven, nine, and eleven each re-explain how it works before making their own point, a reader who's been paying attention rereads the same setup three more times. Either use the mechanism once in full and refer back to it briefly elsewhere ("the same registry from a moment ago"), or find a different piece of real evidence for the later point instead of reusing the first one. A research pass over the codebase (Step 1) should turn up enough distinct mechanisms that no single one has to carry four sections by itself.
- **Unsupported broader implications** — extending a narrow technical finding into a sweeping claim about the industry or the future.
- **Grandiose stakes** — describing an internal engineering fix as though it matters at civilizational scale.
- **Pivot paragraphs** — a paragraph whose only job is to announce a topic change ("Now let's talk about...") rather than carrying content.
- **One-point dilution** — spreading a single idea across three paragraphs that each restate it slightly differently instead of developing it once, densely.
- **Fractal summaries** — summarizing at the end of every section and then summarizing again at the end of the article, recapping the same points multiple times at different zoom levels.
- **Arguing with no one** — defending against an objection nobody raised ("this isn't mainly about...", "to be clear, I'm not saying...", "don't get me wrong") left over from an earlier draft where it may have answered something real.
- **Borrowed authority** — "experts argue," "studies show," "research suggests" used as a vague appeal to credibility with no named source or actual claim behind it. If there's a real source, cite it; if not, don't gesture at one.
- **Vague association** — "associated with X" or "linked to X" standing in for the actual relationship. Say what the relationship is.
- **Plausible invention over a knowledge gap** — hedges like "likely," "as of [date]," "probably" used to paper over missing evidence with an invented-sounding claim, instead of just stating that the evidence isn't available.
- **Avoiding plain verbs** — reaching for "serves as," "features," "boasts," "functions as" where "is" or "has" would just say it.
- **Heading echoed in the first sentence** — restating the heading's own words as the section's opening sentence before adding anything new.

## How to apply

Read the draft once specifically hunting for these patterns, independent of a pass for technical accuracy or voice-matching. Fix an instance the moment it's found rather than cataloguing all of them first — a paragraph rewritten to remove a colon-elaboration tic often needs re-reading anyway to check the fix didn't introduce a different item on this list (a rhetorical question, say, or a new hedge).

A phrase from this list is not automatically wrong if the source author's own corpus genuinely does it (check `style-corpus.md` findings first). The point is to avoid *defaulting* to these patterns as filler, not to ban them outright when the real voice being imitated uses them deliberately and sparingly.
