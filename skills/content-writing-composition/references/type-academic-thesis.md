# Type: Academic/Thesis Writeup

**Signals:** "thesis," "dissertation," "academic manuscript," "research proposal," "introduction/literature review/methodology/results/conclusion chapter," or any request to draft or edit a formal academic manuscript chapter against an institutional thesis guideline, in any language. Distinct from the Conceptual Essay type even though both can be dense and citation-heavy: this type has no personal voice to calibrate against a corpus, follows a chapter structure mandated by the institution rather than one this skill designs, and is judged by a thesis advisor and examiners against a specific written guideline, not by a general reader.

**Grounding:** the institution's own thesis guideline document (if provided) is the primary source of truth for structure, formatting mechanics, and citation style — read it before drafting, the same way Step 1 in `SKILL.md` reads real source code for a technical piece. A project-specific style guide, if one already exists for the document (see below), is the second source of truth and overrides this type's generic guidance where the two conflict; this type file fills gaps the project guide doesn't cover, it doesn't replace it. Never invent a citation, a statistic, a data point, or a methodological justification — everything academic writing states as fact must trace back to a real cited source or the actual research data, with zero exceptions (a stricter version of Step 1's "never fabricate" rule, since a fabricated citation in a thesis is an academic-integrity violation, not just a factual error).

**If a project-specific style guide already exists** (e.g. a `WRITING_GUIDE.md` sitting next to the manuscript, or conventions documented in a project's own context file), treat it as authoritative over this type's generic guidance below — it likely already encodes decisions specific to that institution, that department, or that author's prior audits. Read it in full before touching any manuscript file. If no such guide exists yet, this type's guidance below is the default, and creating a short project-specific guide (mirroring the shape of the example at the bottom of this file) is worth suggesting once real conventions have been established over a few editing rounds, so future sessions don't have to rediscover them.

**Structure:** dictated entirely by the institution's guideline and program-specific template (chapter numbering, required subsections, front/back matter). Do not improvise a structure — follow the mandated one exactly, including sections that look formulaic from a general-writing standpoint (a fixed a/b/c/d list mirrored between the "problem statement" and "research objectives" sections, a funnel-shaped introduction narrowing from broad context to the specific thesis title, a literature-review chapter where every paragraph opens by naming a cited study). These are not AI-writing tells; they are the required genre conventions of the format, and flattening them into "more natural" prose would make the manuscript nonconforming, not better.

**Evidence and citation policy:** every claim of fact needs a citation or a direct link to the study's own verified data/results; every methodological choice needs to be traceable to a real decision the author actually made (not a plausible-sounding justification invented to fill a required subsection). Citation mechanics (author-name form, "et al." vs. the target language's equivalent, in-text vs. narrative citation form, reference list format) follow whatever style the institution mandates (e.g. APA 7th) — check an existing reference list or the guideline document for the exact local convention rather than defaulting to the English-language convention.

**Sanitization:** implementation detail from an underlying real system (raw SQL, literal file paths, database column/table names quoted as if they were the point) generally does not belong in academic narrative prose, even when the research is grounded in a real production system — lift it to the conceptual/statistical level a reader outside the specific codebase can follow, the same instinct as the Conceptual Essay type's code policy, but here the reason is genre register rather than confidentiality. A named data dictionary or formal appendix table is the correct place for literal variable names when the guideline calls for one (a feature dictionary table, for instance) — that's an explicit, sanctioned exception, not a leak. Sensitive fields with no analytical purpose (customer names, staff identifiers) should be excluded from any exported dataset or appendix entirely, not just described euphemistically.

**Tone:** formal academic register in the target language throughout — do not relax into a conversational or blog-like voice even in reflective sections (a genre-appropriate personal-register front-matter page, such as an acknowledgments page, is the one exception). Passive constructions, hedged claims scoped precisely to what the evidence supports, and standard academic transition words are normal here, not filler — the anti-formulaic screen (see `anti-formulaic-writing.md`) still applies, but calibrate its bar to "is this a repeated template doing no new work" rather than "is this too formal," since formality itself is correct for the genre.

**Closing:** each concluding chapter/section states what the specific findings support, scoped to their actual evidentiary strength (correlational vs. causal, single-case-study vs. generalizable, point estimate vs. confidence interval) — this is a required, genre-mandated form of hedging, not the reflexive hedging the anti-formulaic screen warns against elsewhere. Restating headline results in an abstract and again in a conclusion chapter is expected and required by the format; the thing worth catching is restating the same figures a third or fourth time *within* a results/discussion chapter where they were already fully reported a page or two earlier.

## Language-specific patterns worth screening for, distinct from the generic anti-formulaic list

A generic English-oriented pattern-match will miss language-specific versions of the same underlying tells. These are documented from real Indonesian-language thesis auditing, but the principle generalizes to any target language:

- **Template-repeated chapter-closing sentences**, especially in a theory-background or literature-review chapter where every subsection ties its cited theory back to the current research. Doing this once per subsection is expected; doing it with the *exact same sentence shape* every time is the same anaphora/fractal-summary pattern the generic list names, just carried by a language-specific grammatical construction — for example, a cleft construction some languages favor that translates roughly to "[X] is the very thing that founds/grounds [Y]," repeated verbatim across five or more consecutive subsections. Vary the sentence's subject and verb across occurrences (restate in active voice with the tied-back concept as the subject, e.g. "Algorithm X used in this research is built on the foundation of Y" instead of "Y is the very thing that founds algorithm X") rather than cutting the tie-back itself, since linking cited theory back to the study is required content for this chapter type.
  A second, distinct instance of the same pattern: a related-works/literature-review section where every per-study paragraph closes with an identical "strengths, then weaknesses" sentence template (e.g., a fixed pair translating roughly to "This study's advantage is X. Its weakness is Y."). The strengths/weaknesses comparison itself is required content per study; the identical opener repeated across many consecutive studies is the tell. Same fix — rotate the opening construction per occurrence (e.g. alternate "The main strength of this study lies in...", "This research excels at...", "An important contribution of this study is...", and vary the pivot phrase — "On the other hand,", "However,", "Even so,", "Its shortcoming is,") rather than dropping the comparison.
- **"Not X, but Y" scoping clauses used at high frequency.** In careful methodological writing (data leakage caveats, causal-vs-associative language, single-case-study generalization limits) this construction is usually doing real, necessary scoping work, not the reflexive "clear away a misreading first" tic the generic negation-pivot item warns about — don't flag it just because it recurs often in a methodologically careful thesis. Only flag a specific instance if it's setting up a strawman nobody would actually assume, not narrowing a real methodological claim.
- **Cross-reference sentences whose only job is pointing at another chapter** ("detailed further in Chapter III," "as explained in Section 2.2") without adding to the current argument — a genre-specific version of the generic "pivot paragraph" pattern. A formal table/figure reference needed to follow the current passage is legitimate and expected; a bare navigational pointer to content the reader isn't reading right now is not. The test: does removing the clause make the current passage harder to follow, or does it only remove a signpost to content elsewhere?
- **Verbatim metric restatement across chapters beyond what the format requires.** An abstract restating headline results, and a conclusion chapter restating them again, are both required by the genre. A results chapter restating its own headline numbers a second time a few pages later, inside the same chapter, is not required and is worth trimming or replacing with a cross-reference to where the number was already reported in full.
- **Number formatting and locale mechanics** are not stylistic preferences in this type; they're usually institutionally fixed (decimal-comma vs. decimal-point, thousands-separator conventions, a rule against opening a sentence with a digit, a rule against certain words being followed directly by a colon). Check for and follow whatever local numeric/mechanical convention is already established in the document rather than defaulting to a different locale's formatting.

## Common mistakes specific to this type

- Treating a genre-mandated structure (mirrored subsection lists, a funnel-shaped introduction, per-study literature-review paragraphs) as an AI-writing tell and "naturalizing" it — this makes the manuscript nonconforming to its actual required format.
- Skipping the check for an existing project-specific style guide before editing — a project's own `WRITING_GUIDE.md` or equivalent overrides this file's generic defaults, and editing without reading it first risks contradicting decisions already made deliberately.
- Applying the reflexive-hedging item from the generic anti-formulaic checklist to a scoping clause that's actually doing required methodological work (see the "not X, but Y" item above) — don't strip a caveat the evidence genuinely needs just because the construction shows up often.

**Example:** an undergraduate thesis manuscript written in a non-English language, audited chapter-by-chapter against a project-specific `WRITING_GUIDE.md` derived from this skill's `anti-formulaic-writing.md`, which is itself a case of the general pattern above: a project can maintain its own thesis-specific grouping of this skill's checklist, and that project file — not this type's generic defaults — governs when the two overlap.
